## What Is Product Backlog Grooming in monday.com?

Product backlog grooming is the ongoing work of refining items before they enter a sprint — clarifying acceptance criteria, breaking down epics, estimating effort, and reordering by priority. In monday.com, the backlog lives as a board with status, priority, owner, story point, and dependency columns.

Why does grooming matter? Because the alternative is expensive. McKinsey and Co. research shows that the benefits of Agile are undeniable; compared to non-Agile teams, 93% of Agile organizations reported better customer satisfaction, 76% reported better employee engagement, and 93% reported better operational performance. CA Technologies found that Agile teams doing full Scrum have 250% better quality than teams that do no estimating. Estimating is grooming. Skip it, and quality drops.

A clean backlog in monday.com usually has:

- **Item-level detail**: User story, acceptance criteria, design links, and definition of done in the item card.
- **Priority sorting**: Drag-and-drop reordering by impact, effort, or RICE score.
- **Dependency mapping**: Linked items so blockers surface before sprint planning, not during it.
- **Status discipline**: Backlog → Ready → In Sprint → In Progress → Review → Done.

Use the **Kanban view** for grooming sessions. It forces conversation around what's "Ready" versus what still needs work. ## How Do You Run Sprint Planning in monday.com?

Sprint planning is where the groomed backlog becomes a committed scope. In monday.com, this happens on a dedicated Sprint board, usually populated from the backlog via mirror columns or a "Move item" automation.

The mechanics are simple. The discipline is not.

- **Capacity check first**: Use the Workload view to see who has bandwidth across the upcoming two weeks.
- **Pull, don't push**: The team pulls items from the groomed backlog based on velocity, not based on what stakeholders demand.
- **Commit the sprint goal**: Add it as a board description or pinned update — one sentence everyone can recite.
- **Lock the sprint**: Once planning ends, automations flip items from "Backlog" to "In Sprint" and notify the team.

This is also where estimation pays off again. Despite the fact that the traditional Waterfall is still popular, Agile has a higher success rate. Only 9% of Agile-based projects have failed, while for waterfall, it's 29%. That gap widens when teams plan with real data — velocity from the last three sprints, not optimism.

## What Does Sprint Delivery Look Like Inside monday.com?

Sprint delivery is the daily execution — stand-ups, work-in-progress limits, blocker escalation, and progress tracking. In monday.com, you anchor this around three views:

Daily stand-ups in monday.com don't have to be meetings. Many teams use Updates threads on items, combined with an automation that posts a daily status digest to Slack or Teams.

Track blockers as their own status. When an item flips to "Blocked," an automation can:

1. Notify the Scrum Master.
2. Move the item to a "Blockers" group at the top of the board.
3. Start a timer column so blockers don't quietly age.

For engineering-heavy sprints, monday Dev's Git integration ties commits and pull requests to items automatically. Status updates flow from the repository into the sprint board, which kills the "did you push that yet?" Slack thread.

## How Do Burndown Charts and Velocity Work in monday.com?

Burndown charts show remaining work against time. Velocity shows how many points (or items) a team completes per sprint. Both are native to monday Dev and configurable in Work Management via the Chart widget.

Why bother? Because measurement changes behaviour. Agile projects achieve 20% higher ROI. The focus on value delivery drives better financial outcomes. You can't manage delivery you can't see.

Set up the basics:

- **Burndown widget**: Plot story points or item count remaining against sprint days. The ideal line is a straight diagonal; the actual line tells the truth.
- **Velocity chart**: A rolling three-sprint average is more useful than a single sprint's number.
- **Cycle time**: How long an item takes from "In Progress" to "Done." This exposes hidden bottlenecks better than burndown.
- **Cumulative flow diagram**: Width of each status band reveals where work piles up.

## What Makes a Good Sprint Retrospective in monday.com?

A retrospective is the team's standing appointment with continuous improvement. monday.com makes this easy with a dedicated retrospective board where everyone adds items under "Keep," "Stop," "Start," and "Action."

The structure that works:

- **Three columns of feedback**: What went well, what didn't, what we'll try next.
- **Voting**: Use the Vote column so the team prioritises which items to discuss.
- **Action items become real items**: Each retro action gets converted into a backlog item with an owner and a due date. Otherwise it stays a wish.
- **Trend tracking**: Tag retro items by theme. Over six sprints, themes tell you whether the team is actually improving or just complaining in a different format.

Retros are also where the truth about adoption surfaces. 63% report declining software quality, and teams say they're delivering slower, even with better reporting. This paradox—more visibility, worse results—is coming from a misuse of metrics from leadership. Only 15% of business leaders participate meaningfully in Agile practices, and leadership alignment is now one of the top blockers to success. If your retros keep flagging the same upstream issues, the fix isn't in the team — it's in how leadership engages.
