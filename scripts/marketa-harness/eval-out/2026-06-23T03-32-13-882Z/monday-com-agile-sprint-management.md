## How Do You Run Product Backlog Grooming in monday.com?

Backlog grooming in monday.com is a recurring board review, not a separate meeting that produces nothing. The board is the artefact.

A well-groomed backlog answers four questions for every item: *what is it, who owns refinement, what is the priority, and is it ready for sprint?* monday.com lets you encode all four as columns.

Here is a structure that holds up across teams:

- **Item Type**: epic, user story, bug, tech debt, spike.
- **Priority**: P0 through P3, or a custom WSJF score.
- **Status**: new, refined, ready, in sprint, done.
- **Story Points**: numeric column, used later for velocity calculation.
- **Acceptance Criteria**: long-text column linked to a doc.
- **Dependencies**: connect-boards column linking blocking items.

This matters because projects which had clear requirements before starting development were 97% more likely to succeed. Backlog grooming is where requirements get clear. Treat it as a non-negotiable weekly ceremony.

Automations carry the load between sessions. Set up rules like:

- When status changes to "Refined" → notify the Product Owner.
- When an item sits in "New" for 14 days → escalate to the backlog review queue.
- When story points are added → auto-tag "Sized".

The result? Grooming meetings shrink from two hours of debate to thirty minutes of decisions. The board does the prep work.

## What Does Sprint Planning Look Like in monday.com?

Sprint planning in monday.com is a filter, a forecast, and a commitment. You pull "Ready" items from the product backlog into a sprint board, size against historical velocity, and lock the scope.

Most teams structure this with two boards connected by a mirror column:

1. **Product Backlog Board**: all items, all statuses, ranked.
2. **Sprint Board**: only items in the current sprint, with their own swim lanes.

The sprint board uses Kanban view for daily flow and a dashboard for burndown. 60% of Scrum teams release working software every two weeks or faster, so two-week sprints remain the sensible default. Anything longer hides delivery problems. Anything shorter creates ceremony overhead.

Capacity planning is where teams trip up. A six-person team is not 60 story points just because they delivered 60 last sprint. Holidays, support rotations, and meetings eat capacity. monday.com's Workload view shows each engineer's committed hours against their available hours, in real time, so the planning conversation moves from "I think we can do it" to "we have 18 hours of buffer."

For monday Dev users, the sprint planning template includes pre-built status columns, sprint goals, and burndown widgets. Link related items across boards to flag cross-team dependencies before they bite mid-sprint.

## How Do You Track Sprint Execution and Daily Stand-ups?

The board *is* the stand-up. Engineers move their items, the team scans the board, and the conversation focuses on blockers — not status reads.

Three views drive daily execution:

- **Kanban**: shows flow from "To Do" → "In Progress" → "Code Review" → "Done".
- **Workload**: surfaces over-commitment before it becomes a slip.
- **Burndown dashboard**: tracks remaining work against the ideal line.

monday.com automations make this self-running. When an item moves to "Code Review", auto-assign the reviewer. When it stalls in any column for over 48 hours, post a Slack alert to the channel. When the assignee changes, notify the new owner. These small rules eliminate the chasing that usually fills stand-ups.

The productivity case is hard to argue with. Scrum teams report 27% higher productivity compared to non-agile teams, and Scrum teams reduce time-to-market by up to 50% for digital products. Those gains only show up when the daily rhythm is tight — and a well-configured board enforces tightness without micromanagement.

Stand-ups themselves shrink to fifteen minutes. The team answers three questions per person: what moved, what is blocked, what is next. Then they leave.

## What About Sprint Reviews, Retrospectives, and Reporting?

Sprint reviews and retrospectives close the loop. monday.com handles both as structured boards, not as Word docs that go to die in a shared drive.

For the **sprint review**, use a dashboard that pulls completed items, demo links, and stakeholder feedback into one view. Filter by sprint number, group by epic, and walk stakeholders through what shipped.

For the **retrospective**, create a dedicated board with three columns: Keep, Stop, Start. Team members add items anonymously through a monday WorkForm, vote with the emoji column, and the top-voted items become action items with owners and due dates. The retro produces real change because every action is tracked like any other work item.

## What Are the Common Mistakes Teams Make?

Most agile rollouts in monday.com fail for the same handful of reasons:

- **Too many columns**: a 22-column board that no one fills in. Strip it to what you actually use.
- **No automation discipline**: relying on humans to move items. Automate the obvious transitions.
- **Mixed work types on one board**: bugs, features, and operational tickets fighting for the same swim lanes. Separate the boards, link them with mirrors.
- **No definition of done**: items move to "Done" with different bars per engineer. Lock the definition in a board description.
- **Ignoring change management**: rolling out new boards without training. Jeff Sutherland, co-founder of Scrum, estimates that 47 percent of all agile transformations fail — usually because people, not tools, were not brought along.

**Can you use monday.com for Scrum and Kanban at the same time?**
Yes. monday.com supports both frameworks on the same board through different views. Use Kanban view for daily flow and a Timeline or Gantt view for sprint commitments. Many teams run a Scrumban hybrid where the sprint cadence comes from Scrum and the work flow comes from Kanban.

**Should I use monday Work Management or monday Dev for sprint management?**
Choose monday Work Management if your sprints involve cross-functional teams — product, design, marketing, and engineering on shared boards. Pick monday Dev if your team lives in pull requests, bug queues, and Git workflows, since it includes burndown charts, Git integration, and engineering-specific sprint templates out of the box.
